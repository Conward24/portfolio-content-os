Findings from the pilot feed

Why the Daily Pulse reads the same every day

The retrieval runs fine and the ranking does what it was told: sort by top topics, not by recency. That is why the feed trails the news by two to thirteen days, and why the real question is which product this is.

Prepared for Jelani Updated Aug 17 after the call 410 pulses · 10 topics · 40 days Jun 24 – Aug 10, 2026

The headline finding

The pulse trails the news by two to thirteen days

The archive names very few specific companies, but where it does we can check it directly: find when the round actually became public, and compare that to the day the pulse first mentioned it. No inference needed.

Entrackr broke Consint.AI on the morning of August 6th; the pulse named it on the 8th. CARPL.ai was covered across nine outlets from July 23rd and reached the pulse on the 29th. Bunkerhill Health, the largest of the three at $25M led by Khosla, was public on July 16th and took thirteen days.

Bunkerhill and CARPL surfaced on the same day. Two rounds a week apart in the real world arrived together on July 29th, which is the trending mechanic visible in the data: they crossed into top-topic status at the same moment rather than when each broke.

This is the number that decides what can be built. An early-warning product promises a 24 to 48 hour lead. The feed runs a two to thirteen day lag, in the opposite direction.

And that is on purpose. Jelani pulled up the prompt on 17 August. It reads: "Identify the most relevant and recent information prioritized in the last 30 days when available." It sorts by top topics across a 30-day window, not by recency. It is answering "what is being talked about right now," not "what happened yesterday."

So the lag is not retrieval failing to prefer fresh material. The lag is the definition of trending. Something breaks, and days or weeks later it becomes one of the most-discussed things in its topic. The tool is measuring when an event becomes a conversation, which is a real and different thing from when it happened.

n = 2, and that is itself a finding. Four checkable cases in forty days of pulses on the topic explicitly about tracking Series A and B rounds, and one of the four was never covered at all. Every case points the same way, and they agree with every other measurement below.

The short version

Every pulse since June 24 has been archived, so we were able to test the feed instead of guessing about it. Each morning's search returns the best general account of the topic rather than what happened yesterday, and measured against public coverage the feed lands two to thirteen days behind. On the 17th Jelani found the cause in the prompt: it ranks by top topics over a 30-day window. Nothing is broken. What we have is a trend detector, and the decision in front of us is whether that is the product we want.

Two gaps found by hand-checking, 17 August

The investor layer is missing entirely

The pulse covered Bunkerhill Health's Series B. It never said who led it.

Khosla Ventures, Sequoia and Optum Ventures appear nowhere in all 410 pulses. Not once, across every topic, in the whole archive. Khosla led that round; Sequoia, Felicis, Optum Ventures and Y Combinator were all in it, and none of them are named anywhere.

On a topic called tracking venture capital funding and Series A/B investment trends, who is writing the cheques is the signal. The company name is only the headline. This looks like a line in the prompt rather than anything structural.

Emotion scores only exist for fourteen days

Every pulse before 27 July carries sentiment: null and all-zero emotions. So the emotion history is 14 to 15 days per topic, not 40, and any scoring built on it has far less to stand on than the archive size suggests. Worth confirming whether that start date was intended.

It also sets a trap for anyone measuring stability. Run the day-to-day correlation across all forty days and you get 0.512, which reads as "these scores are stable." That is an artifact: twenty-six identical zeros in a row are perfectly correlated with themselves. Run it only on days that carry data and it is −0.012, statistically indistinguishable from noise.

One topic is a real exception on the corrected figure. AI healthcare regulation holds at 0.521, the only one of ten clearly above zero.

What the day-to-day change actually consists of

About 39% of the words are new each day, and almost none of them mean anything

Printing them settles it. A representative day's new vocabulary against the prior week:

administrative, confidence, considerably, drive, efficiency, innovation, looking, potential, premiums, reflecting, stages, startup

That is the same account of the same subject, rewritten. It is also why the emotion scores jitter: they are a deterministic function of text that gets reworded every morning, so they end up measuring the paraphrase rather than the mood.

Some days are genuinely different. 31 July brought assured, isomorphic, labs — real companies, real events. Those are the days worth detecting, and they are a small minority.

Why this happens

Top-topic ranking beats recency, because that is what it was told to do

Ask for the most-discussed items in a 30-day window and the winner is almost always a thorough overview of the subject. It matches the whole query. Yesterday's narrow development matches one corner of it, so it ranks lower until enough other coverage accumulates behind it to make it a top topic in its own right.

Run that same search every morning and the same overview comes back, worded a little differently each time. The process runs daily. The result doesn't change daily — until the underlying conversation does, which is exactly the two-to-six-day lag.

In Jelani's framing on the call: this is sorting by top topics, the way you would sort a feed by "top" rather than "recent." Both are legitimate sorts. They are just different products.

The good news

Your index isn't stale

About half the dated facts in the pulses point to events from inside the observation window — June 2026 or later — and 18% come from the same month the email was sent. The fresh material is reaching your index and does surface sometimes.

That's why this is a tuning problem and not a rebuild.

The fork

This is a positioning decision, not a repair

Nothing below is broken. The question is which of two products this is, and they need different query layers. Jelani, on the call: "We never sold ourselves as breaking news. We're not reporters. We can't break the news." That is a fair description of what exists today, and the trend detector is the cheaper, more defensible product. Items 1 and 2 only apply if the answer is the other one.

  1. Only if we want early warning — give the search a time window

    Restrict retrieval to the last 24–72 hours, or run two searches: one for what is new, a small one for background. This is most of the change, and it turns the product into something different from what it is today.

  2. Only if we want early warning — change the question

    Today it answers "what are the top topics of the last 30 days." It would need to answer "what changed since yesterday," and be allowed to say "not much." Quiet days are what make the loud ones mean something.

  3. Worth doing either way — name the investors

    Whoever led the round, and who else participated. It is absent from every pulse in the archive and it is the highest-value field on a funding topic. Almost certainly one line in the prompt.

  4. Worth doing either way — send us what the search already found

    The date range it covered, how many documents came back, which publications they came from, and the links. This is the one item that helps both products, and it is not a new feature: it is keeping what the search already returns.

Why step 3 is smaller than it looks

It hands us the missing half of the signal score for free

Once the search is scoped to a 24-hour window, the number of documents it returns is the mention volume, and the distinct publications are the source breadth. Those two numbers are 45% of the signal-strength score, and they're the item both specs list as the one thing still to build.

Nobody has to build a counter. We just have to stop discarding what the search already hands back. The links are the other half of it — they're what lets the newsletter say "we flagged this on the 3rd, it was covered on the 6th."

One change solves four things:
→ a real daily observation
→ mention volume
→ source breadth
→ source links

The version worth considering

The lag is the signal, not the defect

Michael, on the call: "It is nice to know when something shifts from this is just reported news to this is now trending news." That is the product hiding in plain sight.

Knowing an event happened is a commodity; there are a hundred outlets for that. Knowing the exact moment it crosses from reported to discussed is not, and it is precisely what this pipeline already detects. Measured against public coverage, that crossing took two days for Consint.AI, six for CARPL.ai and thirteen for Bunkerhill. That spread is information: it says how fast a thing is travelling, and the slowest of the three was the biggest round.

It is also the better fit for how we actually use the feed. Publishing the day news breaks means competing with reporters. Publishing as something becomes a conversation is when a point of view gets read.

Same pipeline, different claim:
→ not "we saw it first"
→ "we saw it become a conversation"
→ and the gap to public coverage is measurable, every time

What we're not asking for

  • Not a new crawler. The index is fine.
  • Not a new data source.
  • Not more fields on the current email. We asked for that before and it was the wrong ask — adding columns to a topic overview just produces a better-labelled topic overview.
  • Not a change to the emotion scoring. It is already deterministic: a word library with the calculation in JavaScript, so the same text always produces the same score. That is the right design. The day-to-day randomness we measured comes from the summary being reworded each morning, not from the scorer.

What happens next

Every pulse is archived on our side, so the day this change lands we can compare before and after immediately — same topics, same pipeline. We'll know inside a week whether the scoring layer starts working.

Ten topics is a pilot configuration, not a limit, so nothing here depends on that number.
