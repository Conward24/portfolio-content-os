Findings from the pilot feed

Why the Daily Pulse reads the same every day

The daily retrieval is running fine. The problem is what it brings back — and three changes in the query layer fix it.

Prepared for Jelani 410 pulses · 10 topics · 40 days Jun 24 – Aug 10, 2026

The short version

Every pulse since June 24 has been archived, so we were able to test the feed instead of guessing about it. What the archive shows is that each morning's search returns the best general explanation of the topic rather than what happened yesterday — because "most relevant" means exactly that when the search has no sense of time. Your index is fine and the fresh material is in it. It's losing the ranking.

What we found

The pulse describes the topic. It doesn't report the day.

Six checks, all pointing the same direction. The last one is the most important, because it's the one that shows the engine works.

Why this happens

Relevance and recency are pulling in opposite directions

Search a broad topic like "Medicaid reimbursement for doula services" and the best match by relevance will almost always be a thorough, well-written overview. It covers the whole subject, so it scores highest against the query. Yesterday's news story covers one narrow development, so it scores lower — even though it's the only thing that's actually new.

Run that same search every morning and the same overview comes back, worded a little differently each time. The process runs daily. The result doesn't change daily.

The good news

Your index isn't stale

About half the dated facts in the pulses point to events from inside the observation window — June 2026 or later — and 18% come from the same month the email was sent. The fresh material is reaching your index and does surface sometimes.

That's why this is a tuning problem and not a rebuild.

The fix

Three changes, all in the query layer

  1. Give the search a time window

    Restrict retrieval to the last 24–72 hours. Or run two searches: one for what's new, a small one for background context. This alone is most of the fix.

  2. Change the question you're asking the model

    Right now it's effectively answering "describe this topic." It needs to answer "what changed in this topic since yesterday" — and be allowed to say "not much." A pulse that honestly reports a quiet day is worth more than one that restates the primer, because quiet days are what make the loud ones mean something.

  3. Send us what the search already found

    The date range it covered, how many documents came back, which publications they came from, and the links.

Why step 3 is smaller than it looks

It hands us the missing half of the signal score for free

Once the search is scoped to a 24-hour window, the number of documents it returns is the mention volume, and the distinct publications are the source breadth. Those two numbers are 45% of the signal-strength score, and they're the item both specs list as the one thing still to build.

Nobody has to build a counter. We just have to stop discarding what the search already hands back. The links are the other half of it — they're what lets the newsletter say "we flagged this on the 3rd, it was covered on the 6th."

One change solves four things:
→ a real daily observation
→ mention volume
→ source breadth
→ source links

What we're not asking for

  • Not a new crawler. The index is fine.
  • Not a new data source.
  • Not more fields on the current email. We asked for that before and it was the wrong ask — adding columns to a topic overview just produces a better-labelled topic overview.

What happens next

Every pulse is archived on our side, so the day this change lands we can compare before and after immediately — same topics, same pipeline. We'll know inside a week whether the scoring layer starts working.

Ten topics is a pilot configuration, not a limit, so nothing here depends on that number.

Appendix — the technical version

For the record, the six checks in their proper names. All computed from the archived pulses; nothing is estimated.

  • Text similarity by lag — mean Jaccard similarity of content words between pulse pairs on the same topic, bucketed by days apart. 0.187 at lag 1d → 0.144 at lag 30d, n = 385 pairs at lag 1.
  • Same-day language — regex over all 410 summaries for today | yesterday | this week | this morning | breaking | just announced | hours ago. Zero matches.
  • Citation recency — newest past month cited per summary (forward-looking policy effective dates excluded; one summary cites January 2028). 18% same month, 50% within one month, 50% two to seven months old.
  • Emotion persistence — lag-1 autocorrelation of emotion polarity per topic. Mean r₁ = 0.022, z = 1.09 against the white-noise null; 6 of 10 topics positive, 4 negative.
  • Index advance — correlation between email order and newest past fact cited: 0.000. Correlation with lag: +0.122 (falling further behind over time).
  • The control topic — regulatory-developments-for-artificial-intelligence-in-healthcare: r₁ = 0.498 (highest), sentiment sd 0.894 (highest), largest sentiment shift. The one topic with real news flow is the one that behaves like signal.
